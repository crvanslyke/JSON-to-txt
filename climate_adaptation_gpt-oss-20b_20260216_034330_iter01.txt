Adapting Your Rice‑Crawfish Farm to a Changing Climate
Prepared by Louisiana Sea Grant – Acadiana Farming Outreach
 1. How the Weather is Shifting in Acadiana
- Rainfall patterns are becoming less predictable.  Some years bring heavy downpours that can flood fields early; others deliver long dry spells that dry rice paddies faster than usual.
- Summer temperatures are climbing.  Heat stress can shorten the rice growing season and reduce crawfish survival in warm water.
- Storm frequency is increasing.  Hurricanes, tropical storms and even intense thunderstorms can damage infrastructure and alter water tables overnight.
- Sea‑level rise is altering groundwater levels in low‑lying areas, making it harder to keep fields flooded or dry as needed.
These changes mean the classic “plant rice → flood → harvest → rest, then raise crawfish” cycle is now happening on a different timetable.
 2. Practical Adaptation Strategies You Can Try Today
 Goal  What to Do  Why It Helps
 Keep rice paddies on schedule  • Use early‑maturing rice varieties (e.g., “Gulf F1” or “Red River 2”) that finish in 70–80 days instead of the usual 90. <br>• Plant a double‑crop of rice if you have sufficient water and field space.  Shorter growing time gives you a buffer against late‑season heat or early storms.
 Control water more precisely  • Install a simple rain‑water harvesting system (e.g., 1 m² catch basin) to capture runoff for later use. <br>• Set up drainage tiles or a shallow trench system to avoid water‑logging during unexpected storms.  You’ll have the ability to lower or raise water levels quickly, protecting both crops and crawfish.
 Protect crawfish from heat  • Raise the water level by 10–15 cm during the hottest months; cooler water helps crawfish survive. <br>• Use shade cloth or local brush to reduce pond surface temperature if you have the space.  Keeps crawfish healthy and reduces mortality that can wipe out a season’s income.
 Diversify your production  • Grow cover crops (e.g., millet, sorghum) in the fall to improve soil structure and provide extra forage for crawfish. <br>• Consider adding a small pond segment for a different species of crayfish that tolerates higher temperatures.  Multiple income streams cushion against one crop’s failure.
 Use low‑cost monitoring tools  • Keep a simple soil moisture chart or use a handheld meter to decide when water can be let in or drained. <br>• Mark critical thresholds on a yardstick (e.g., “if soil is drier than 50 % moisture, fill the field”).  Quick decisions based on data can prevent costly mistakes.
 Build a resilient network  • Join your local rice‑crawfish co‑op or farmers’ group to share water rights, storm‑damage insurance, and equipment. <br>• Attend Sea Grant workshops or “farm‑to‑table” town halls for up‑to‑date climate info.  Collective action spreads risk and knowledge.
 3. Timing Your Flooding & Harvest: A Simple Decision Guide
 Condition  Action  Tip
 Heavy rain forecasted within 48 h  Delay flooding until after the storm if possible.  Let the field drain naturally; this reduces excess water that can drown rice seedlings.
 Soil moisture below 40 %  Flood earlier than usual, or add a small water reserve from your catch basin.  Young rice seedlings need 5–10 cm of standing water for optimal growth.
 Temperature above 35 °C (95 °F) in the last week of rice  Harvest earlier by 3–5 days to avoid heat stress.  Rice that is harvested too late may have lower grain quality and higher moisture content, making storage harder.
 Crawfish water temperature rises above 28 °C (82 °F)  Increase pond depth by 10–15 cm or cover part of the surface.  Cooler water slows crawfish metabolism and reduces disease risk.
 Storm or hurricane warning  Drain rice paddies completely and secure structures; consider moving crawfish to a protected pond or raising them in smaller containers if feasible.  Prevent loss of fish and crop damage from wind‑borne debris.
Quick Reference Checklist (for the farmer’s clipboard)
1. Check rain forecast → If heavy, adjust flood schedule.
2. Measure soil moisture → Decide on filling or draining.
3. Record water depth (cm) → Target 5–10 cm for rice, 30–40 cm for crawfish.
4. Note temperature → Adjust harvest or pond depth accordingly.
 4. A Few Words of Encouragement
You’ve been farming here for generations, learning the rhythms of Acadiana’s waterways and seasons. Climate change is a new variable—one that you can bring into your familiar system with small, practical steps. The strategies above are rooted in what we’ve seen work locally: shorter rice varieties, better water control, and a bit of diversification.
If you try one or two ideas this season, keep notes on what worked and what didn’t. Share those stories with your neighbors—often the best solution is a shared one.
Remember:
- Adaptation is incremental. Small changes today build resilience for tomorrow.
- Your experience matters. The knowledge you bring to the table is the first line of defense against uncertainty.
